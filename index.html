The piece of software at the centre of every stock exchange.

Two rules decide every trade

An order book is the list of everyone waiting to trade: buyers (bids) on one side, sellers (asks) on the other. When a new order arrives, the engine matches it against the other side using just two rules, in this order.

Rule 1 — Price

Best price goes first

A new buyer trades with the cheapest seller available; a new seller trades with the highest buyer. The price that was already resting in the book is the one you pay.

So a buyer willing to pay 102 still trades at 101 if someone is already selling there — they get the better deal, not the worse one.

Buy 40 @ 102 → fills 25 @ 101 first then 15 @ 102

Rule 2 — Time

Same price? First in line wins

When several orders sit at the same price, the one that arrived earliest trades first. Each price level is a queue — join the back, move up as those ahead get filled.

This is what "price–time priority" means, and it's why being fast to the exchange is worth money.

#7 and #8 both sell @ 100 Buy 15 → fills #7 fully, then #8

    How it's built

    The whole point of the project was to pick the right data structures and prove the choice with numbers. Here's the inside of the Java engine.

    The book is three data structures working together, each chosen so the hot operations stay cheap:

    Two sorted maps of price levels — one for bids, one for asks (TreeMap<Price, Deque>). A sorted map hands back the best price instantly, which is the single most common question the engine asks.

    A FIFO queue at each price (ArrayDeque). Orders at the same price are served oldest-first, so time priority is just "take the front of the queue". Partial fills shrink the front order and leave everyone else's place untouched.

    A hash index from order id to order (HashMap), so cancelling an order is a direct lookup instead of a scan.

    Matching is then a tight loop: look at the best opposite price, trade against the order at the front of its queue, repeat until the incoming order is filled or no price crosses. A limit order stops when the price no longer crosses and rests in the book; a market order takes whatever it can and never rests.

    Every rule is pinned down by 20 unit tests that run in GitHub Actions on each push, and a benchmark drives millions of random orders through the book to measure throughput and tail latency honestly, rather than guessing.
